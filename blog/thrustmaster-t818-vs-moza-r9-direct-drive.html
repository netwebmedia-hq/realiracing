The jump from belt-driven to direct drive changes how a sim feels. I race on a MOZA R9, which sits in the ~9 N·m range and costs roughly $1000 USD. The Thrustmaster T818 occupies similar territory: mid-tier direct drive at ~8 N·m and a similar price point. Both claim to deliver genuine FFB granularity and real-world steering feel. The question is whether one delivers better value, build longevity, or simply suits your rig and driving style better. This guide draws on published specs and common setup practice, not a hands-on test of every product. The differences are specific enough to matter.

Torque and power delivery

The MOZA R9 produces ~9 N·m while the T818 pushes ~8 N·m. On paper that is a small gap. In heavy cornering or rally driving, the R9's extra headroom should be noticeable. Grip-loss bumps at Laguna Seca or Darlington may hit harder through the MOZA. The T818 should not feel weak, but it will clip sooner if you run high FFB gain in a heavy car like the Mercedes AMG GT3 or Porsche 963.

Thrustmaster built the T818 to scale torque smoothly across its range. Smaller steering inputs feel precise. The R9's torque curve also scales well, though it edges the T818 slightly in mid-range bite. For oval racing where small corrections matter, both are capable. For road racing on high-grip circuits with consistent heavy loading, the R9's extra torque reserves mean you can push gain a notch higher without clipping.

Build quality and longevity

The MOZA R9 wheelbase uses sealed bearings and a robust gear design. Thrustmaster's T818 employs a Japanese-sourced direct drive motor and similarly solid construction. Real-world reliability data for both is still building. Early T818 units shipped with firmware quirks that Thrustmaster patched.

Cooling matters for sustained endurance racing. The R9's heatsink sits on the motor housing and dissipates heat during long stints. The T818 also includes cooling provisions, though reports suggest it runs slightly warmer under sustained heavy load. For four-hour endurance races with high FFB gain, the R9's thermal headroom gives peace of mind. Both should handle most amateur league schedules without thermal throttling.

Software, firmware and ecosystem

MOZA's suite includes their standalone wheelbase software, which lets you tweak FFB damping, friction, and inertia without touching your rig's control panel. You can flip settings between cars or drivers in seconds. Thrustmaster's ecosystem is mature and broader. Their proprietary driver software integrates with a large library of games outside iRacing. If you play other sims, Thrustmaster's ecosystem may feel more cohesive.

iRacing support is native on both. The T818 works out of the box; same with the R9. Firmware updates roll out periodically. Thrustmaster has a longer track record of supporting older wheels, whereas MOZA is newer but has been responsive to user feedback. If longevity in the iRacing ecosystem is your concern, both are safe picks, though Thrustmaster's installed user base is larger.

Motor noise trade-off

Direct drive wheels do generate more motor noise than belt-driven bases. Both the T818 and R9 emit a faint whirring under load. If you share an apartment or live in noise-sensitive spaces, expect neighbor complaints during late-night practice. This is not unique to either wheelbase, but it is worth testing in person.

Wheel compatibility

The MOZA R9 uses MOZA's proprietary QR system and accepts MOZA rims. I run the MOZA CS V2 steering wheel, which bolts directly to my R9 with no adapters. The ecosystem is growing. Aftermarket manufacturers now sell QR-compatible rims, but the selection still lags behind Fanatec's ecosystem. Thrustmaster uses their established QR design. Legacy Thrustmaster wheels fit the T818. The installed base of T818-compatible aftermarket rims is smaller than Fanatec CSL DD compatibility, but larger than MOZA's current offering.

If you already own a Thrustmaster wheel, the T818 is a direct upgrade path. If you own a MOZA wheel, the R9 is your natural progression. If you are starting fresh, wheel choice is a toss-up between both brands' current offerings. Neither wheelbase has a decisive advantage in rim availability, though Fanatec CSL DD and higher-tier MOZA units enjoy broader third-party support.

Price and value

The R9 retails for roughly $1000 USD. The T818 sits at a similar price, though regional pricing and bundle deals vary. Neither wheelbase includes pedals or a button box. A complete beginner rig with the R9 requires separate investment in the MOZA CRP2 load-cell pedals (~$300 USD) and a cockpit. The T818 pairs well with Thrustmaster's own pedal lineup, which spans budget to load-cell options. Total entry cost is comparable for both ecosystems.

Value hinges on your intent. If you race iRacing exclusively and want proven integration, both deliver. The R9 edges ahead on torque and thermal performance. The T818 edges ahead on broader ecosystem compatibility outside iRacing and legacy wheel support. For pure value in the iRacing context, the gap is marginal. Both are solid purchases that will not need replacement within three to five years of regular use.

Real-world feel in iRacing

Expect the R9 to convey track texture clearly. Kerb impacts come through sharp, and grip loss registers as rapid weight transfer feedback. The T818 delivers this same feedback, though some users report slightly softer peak torque at the extremes. In practice, both wheelbases resolve iRacing's FFB output accurately enough that driver skill and setup tuning matter far more than the wheelbase choice. The delta is real but small.

Where the R9 distinguishes itself is in sustained high-load scenarios. Road America's fast corners or the Nordschleife's Mulsanne-style straights demand constant steering effort. The R9 should stay responsive and cool. The T818 remains capable but works harder. For oval racing where steering input is smaller and more discrete, both wheelbases are functionally identical in feel.

Recommendation

Choose the R9 if you want slightly more torque, proven iRacing optimization, and a growing ecosystem you can expand into. Choose the T818 if you value Thrustmaster's broader gaming ecosystem, legacy wheel compatibility, or already own Thrustmaster pedals and software. Both are mature, reliable direct drive wheelbases that will transform your iRacing experience compared to entry-level gear. The choice between them is preference, not necessity. I race on the R9 and can recommend it without hesitation. The T818 is equally valid for the same budget.

FAQ

Is the MOZA R9 actually stronger than the Thrustmaster T818?

The R9 produces roughly 9 N·m versus the T818's 8 N·m. In heavy cars or high-FFB gain setups, the extra headroom is noticeable. For most road racing and daily practice, the difference is too small to matter. It becomes relevant only if you run at the top of gain range consistently.

Can I use my old Thrustmaster wheel on the T818?

Yes. The T818 uses Thrustmaster's standard QR design, so legacy wheels and rims fit directly. The MOZA R9 uses its own QR system, so you cannot use old Thrustmaster rims on it without an adapter. This matters if you already own Thrustmaster gear.

Which one is quieter during driving?

Both generate motor noise under steering load. The R9 and T818 are comparably quiet. Neither is silent. If noise is a real concern, direct drive is inherently louder than belt-driven alternatives. Test either in person before committing if you live in noise-sensitive spaces.

Will either wheelbase have thermal issues during endurance racing?

Both are designed for sustained use. The R9's heatsink is slightly more robust. During four-plus hour races at high FFB gain, the R9 stays cooler. The T818 handles long races fine but runs warmer. In amateur league racing, thermal throttling is unlikely on either, but the R9 has more thermal headroom if you push hard for hours.
