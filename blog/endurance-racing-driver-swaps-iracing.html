Endurance Racing and Driver Swaps on iRacing: A Practical Guide

I race on a MOZA R9 direct drive wheelbase, and what matters most is having gear that is honest about what the car is doing. Team endurance races on iRacing add a layer most sprint racers never deal with: handing a car with a specific fuel load, tire age, and setup quirks over to a teammate who has to adapt to it immediately.

I want to be upfront that none of this is a shortcut; every point below is something to practice deliberately rather than something that clicks automatically after enough races. If it feels awkward the first few times you try it, that's normal, not a sign it doesn't work.

Why this actually matters over a full race

It's easy to underrate a race-craft skill like this because it doesn't show up as directly in a single hot lap the way a setup change does; over a full race distance, the drivers who've internalized this consistently gain more positions and avoid more incidents than the ones relying purely on outright pace.

What to do

  1. Agree on stint length and fuel strategy with your team before the race, not mid-race under pressure
  2. Leave clear notes for your teammate about anything unusual with the car's balance during your stint
  3. Practice the actual driver-change procedure in a private session so it isn't the first time either of you has done it during a real race
  4. Build in a buffer for the incoming driver's first lap or two back up to pace, as a cold driver on a car they did not just spend the last hour in needs a moment to recalibrate.

I'd resist the temptation to try to apply all of these at once in your very next race. Pick one, focus on it deliberately for a session or two until it feels close to automatic, then layer in the next one. Trying to fix everything simultaneously usually means none of it actually sticks.

Mistakes that keep costing time

  • Assuming your teammate knows exactly how the car is handling without actually telling them
  • Underestimating how much slower the first lap or two back on track is for a driver swapping into a car mid-stint
  • Poor fuel or tire communication leading to an unplanned extra pit stop

What's useful about naming these specific mistakes is that they're genuinely visible on a replay: watch your own footage back after a race specifically looking for these patterns rather than just watching for overtakes and incidents in general. It's a much more targeted way to actually improve than just racing more laps and hoping consistency arrives on its own.

Pro Tip

Do at least one full practice driver swap with your team before race day; the mechanics of the swap itself (who's ready when, how the handoff is communicated) benefit enormously from a dry run.

How your gear fits into this

None of this is really about hardware; it is a driving-skill topic more than an equipment one. But I will say that running a direct drive wheel like the MOZA R9 and a proper load-cell pedal set like the MOZA CRP2 makes the feedback loop tighter: mistakes are more obvious the moment they happen, which speeds up how quickly a technique like this actually sinks in, compared to hardware that smooths over or delays that feedback.

That said, I'd be cautious about blaming (or crediting) hardware for something that's fundamentally a driving habit. Drivers on entry-level wheels can execute this cleanly, and drivers on expensive direct-drive setups can still get it wrong; the equipment changes how clearly you can feel the result, not whether you're actually doing it correctly in the first place.

How this changes as you move up in car and series class

What works at a beginner level often needs refinement as you move into faster, higher-grip cars and more competitive splits. The margin for a slightly late reaction shrinks as closing speeds increase, and fields with tighter lap-time spreads punish a small technique gap more severely than a field with a wide range of pace. I'd revisit this specific skill every time you make a significant jump in car class rather than assuming what worked in a slower car transfers unchanged.

Moving between the MX-5 and faster GT machinery, a technique that felt completely sufficient at lower speeds can need real refinement once closing speeds and grip levels go up. That's not a failure of the original technique, just a reminder that 'good enough' is relative to the class you're actually racing.

How I'd measure whether it's actually working

The honest test is not a single lap time but whether your incident count drops and your finishing position in races becomes more consistent over several weeks. A technique like this rarely shows up as a dramatic one-race transformation; it shows up as a gradual reduction in the same avoidable mistakes happening race after race. I'd track your own incident log specifically for the pattern this technique is meant to fix, rather than judging progress purely by gut feeling.

A realistic timeline for building this habit

I'd give this a genuine month of regular racing before judging whether it is working; that is roughly how long it can take to stop consciously thinking about it and start doing it automatically. Anything shorter than that and you're really just in the awkward, deliberate-practice phase, which is a normal and necessary step but not yet the finished skill.

It's also worth being patient with yourself through the plateaus. Skill development on iRacing rarely improves in a straight line; you'll likely notice a period where it feels like nothing is changing, followed by a noticeable jump once the habit actually consolidates. That pattern is normal and doesn't mean the practice isn't working.

This connects directly to the Porsche 963 GTP: the two skills build on each other more than they seem to at first, and I'd recommend working on both roughly in parallel rather than treating them as fully separate topics.

FAQ

Does this technique apply to every car on iRacing?

The general principle applies broadly, though the specific feel and margin for error changes with the car's grip level and weight distribution.

How long does it take to build this skill?

It varies by driver, but deliberate, focused practice sessions build it faster than just racing and hoping it improves passively.

Do I need expensive hardware to practice this?

No, this is a driving-skill topic, not a hardware one. It's more about repetition and feedback than what wheel or pedals you're using.

Where should I practice this before trying it in a real race?

An offline practice session or a lower-stakes league race is a safer place to build the habit before relying on it somewhere that actually matters to your rating.
