Choosing Gear Ratios for a Track You Don't Know Yet

Walking into a new track on iRacing is brutal for setup. You don't know the real braking zones, you're guessing on corner speed, and your throttle application is tentative. Gearing makes it worse: pick a ratio that's too aggressive and you'll hit the rev limiter on straights while losing mid-range torque in technical sections. Pick one too conservative and you'll bleed speed on acceleration zones you haven't fully mapped yet.

The real skill is starting safe, then letting the track teach you where to tighten the ratio. You won't know your actual top speed in first session. You won't know if you're over-braking or under-braking. And you definitely won't know if that corner feels slow because your line is wrong or because your engine is running out of revs. This post walks through the setup process I recommend: start conservative, identify where you're actually losing time, then adjust.

Why Standard Gearing Data Lies on Unfamiliar Tracks

Setup shops and shared setups are built on laps from people who know the track. When someone shares a Ferrari 488 GT3 setup for Suzuka, those gear ratios assume you're hitting specific apex speeds, holding throttle at consistent points, and braking at established markers. If you don't know Suzuka, your actual corner speed might be 10–15 km/h slower. Your braking point might be 100 meters earlier. That gearing that worked for the builder will feel either neutered (if too tall) or frustrating (if too short).

The opposite also happens: new drivers inherit aggressive gearing and think they're weak on acceleration when really they're just not at the throttle hard enough yet because they're nervous about the corner exit. The gear ratio becomes a distraction from learning the actual line.

Truth

Gearing should be the last thing you blame when something feels wrong. Line, braking point, and throttle application come first. A gear ratio won't fix a 5-degree steering input error.

Start with the baseline, then go slightly tall.

Most stock or baseline setups come pre-geared for a middle-of-the-road driver at that track. On a 488 GT3, that might mean ratios dialed around ~9 N·m of torque output, with a final drive built for maybe 295 km/h top speed on a fast circuit like Monza or Le Mans. That's a safe assumption if you know the track. If you don't, run 1-2% taller on the final drive than the stock recommendation.

Why? Because it's easier to add revs later than to crush your motivation by hitting the limiter constantly in your first five laps. A taller final drive also reduces engine braking slightly, which helps when you're learning brake points because you won't have as much compression helping you slow down; you have to feel the actual braking requirement. Once you know the track intimately, you can tighten the ratio to recover that acceleration and use engine braking as a tool.

  • Increase final drive by ~1–2% (most sims let you dial this in 0.1% increments)
  • Keep individual gear ratios stock initially; don't remix the whole box.
  • Test for limiter hits on the fastest sections of your first practice session
  • If you're not hitting limiter anywhere, you're safe; if you're hitting it in low-speed zones, you went too tall

Map your actual throttle zones; that is where gearing reveals itself.

Spend your first two practice sessions just learning where the track makes you want more acceleration. Use a telemetry overlay and pay attention to: Where are you flat-out? Where do you lift mid-corner? Where do you re-apply throttle gradually? That mental map is worth more than any shared setup.

Here's what to listen for: In second or third gear through a medium-speed corner, is the engine helping you accelerate or fighting you? If the revs feel dead and the car doesn't respond, that usually means you're in the wrong gear or your corner speed is too low for the ratio. If the engine is wound tight and accelerating smoothly, you've found the sweet spot for that section.

Red-Line Zones: Where to Tighten the Ratio

After three sessions, you'll have muscle memory on the track. You'll know roughly where you brake, where you apex, and where you're at full throttle. Now look for the straights or acceleration zones where you're regularly hitting the rev limiter early. That's your signal that the final drive is too tall.

If it's the main straight and you're limited before the braking zone, tighten the final drive by ~0.5-1% and test again. If it's a short acceleration zone between corners, you might tighten the lower gears (2nd and 3rd) instead to improve mid-range punch without over-revving on the straights. If a section needs brisk acceleration out of a chicane but does not carry to high speed (Spa's Eau Rouge is one example), leave the final drive alone and try tightening 3rd gear by one tooth.

Diagnosis

Red-line doesn't always mean "too tall." Sometimes it means you're committed to acceleration at full throttle, which is correct. The question is: are you reaching your target corner speed, or are you over-revving because the corner itself isn't as fast as the gear ratio assumes?

Under-Rev Zones: When You're Lugging the Engine

Conversely, if you're in a medium-speed corner (third or fourth gear) and the engine feels strained, like it's struggling to respond to throttle input, you might have gone too tall on the final drive. The engine is spinning below its power band, and you lose responsiveness.

This is less obvious than hitting the limiter, so use telemetry to check RPM relative to gear. If you're in 4th at 4,000 revs and the car feels sluggish, but the motor makes peak power around 7,500 revs, you likely need to shorten the final drive by 0.5-1% or lower that specific gear ratio.

  1. Open telemetry overlay and note RPM in each gear on your normal driving line
  2. Compare to the engine's power curve (usually available in the setup menu or on iRacing's car pages)
  3. If you're chronically 1,500+ revs below peak power in 3rd or 4th, tighten the final drive by 0.5%
  4. Re-test and measure lap time delta; even a fraction of a second improvement is a signal you got closer.

The One-Variable Rule: Change Only Final Drive First

When you're learning a new track, resist the urge to remix the entire gear box. Don't change 1st, 2nd, 3rd, and 4th all at once. Change the final drive (the master ratio affecting all gears proportionally), test for one or two sessions, then decide if you need to trim individual gears. This helps you avoid chasing your tail on an unfamiliar circuit.

Changing individual gears makes sense once you know the track and you're optimizing for a specific car or race strategy (e.g., taller 5th for a long drag, shorter 2nd for a tight hairpin-heavy circuit). But in your first five sessions, the final drive is the only lever you need.

When to Stop Fiddling and Just Drive

Here's the hard part: knowing when you've got it right. A good rule: if you're within 0.3 seconds of your personal best lap on a track, and you're not hitting the limiter and you're not lugging the engine, the gearing is good enough. At that point, continuing to adjust does more harm than good because you lose focus on actually learning the track.

The last 2 to 3 tenths usually come from line refinement, brake timing, and throttle modulation rather than gearing. Once the gear ratio stops bottlenecking your acceleration or causing constant limiter hits, move on.

Mindset

If you've adjusted gearing three times in a session and you're still unsure, the issue isn't gearing. Stop and work on your braking points or entry speed instead. Gearing should fade into the background once it's close.

FAQ

Should I use shared setups or baseline setups when learning a new track?

(FAQ) Baseline is safer for your first session because it is un-optimized, making no assumptions about your skill or style. Shared setups assume you know the track, so they often carry aggressive gearing. Start with baseline, use one practice session to learn the layout, then carefully adopt a shared setup's gearing if you switch to a tuned base.

How much should I adjust the final drive at a time?

Start with 0.5% increments. A 0.5% change is usually subtle enough that you'll feel the difference (or not) over 2–3 laps. A 2% change all at once can mask whether you went in the right direction. Once you're in the ballpark, you can step down to 0.2% tweaks.

What if the shared setup's gearing feels perfect but I'm still slow?

The gearing is probably fine. You're slow because your line, braking point, or throttle application doesn't match the original driver's. Gearing is maybe 1–2% of your lap time if it's wrong; everything else is the 98%. Focus on replicating the setup driver's telemetry lap if available, or chase their apex speeds and brake markers.

Is engine braking affected by gear ratio changes?

Yes. A taller final drive reduces engine braking; a shorter ratio increases it. If you go too tall and engine braking disappears, you'll feel like the car is coasting more on corner entry; that can make braking feel harder to judge. This is why a slightly-too-tall ratio can actually help learning: you're forced to rely on the actual brakes, not compression, to slow down.
