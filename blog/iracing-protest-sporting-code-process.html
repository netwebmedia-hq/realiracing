iRacing Protest and Sporting Code: The Complete Process

The first time I filed a protest in iRacing, I wasn't sure if I was wasting my time. A driver spun me out, clearly on purpose, and I watched my iRating evaporate while his rating stayed intact. That's when I learned that iRacing does not punish drivers based on what happens in-race alone; there is a formal process, evidence requirements, and a sporting code that governs everything from intentional wrecking to brake-checking to language in the driver comments.

This guide walks you through exactly what the protest system is, when it's worth filing, what iRacing looks for during investigation, and what the sporting code penalties mean for your license and future races. I'm writing this from the perspective of someone who's filed protests that were upheld, had protests filed against me, and learned what actually moves the needle with iRacing officials.

What Is the iRacing Protest System?

iRacing's protest system is a formal complaint mechanism that allows drivers to report conduct they believe violated the sporting code. It is not a real-time moderation system; stewards do not watch every lap of every race. Instead, you file a protest with evidence (typically a replay file and description of the incident), and iRacing's officials review your case and the sporting code to decide if a violation occurred.

The key distinction: the protest system is about conduct violations, not racing contact. A clean race where I accidentally punt someone into T1 because I braked late is not grounds for a protest. But if that same driver then intentionally spins me back three laps later as payback, that is a conduct violation and retaliatory driving, which is protestable.

Time Limit Matters

You can only protest a driver's conduct within a few hours of the race ending. Don't wait days to file; the system closes protests relatively quickly. Check the iRacing website for the exact protest window for the series you raced.

The Sporting Code: The Rules That Matter

The iRacing sporting code covers a lot of ground, but the most protest-worthy violations fall into a few clear categories. Understanding these helps you know when a protest is actually justified and when you're just upset about hard racing.

  • Intentional wrecking or ramming: Deliberately using your car as a weapon to spin or punt another driver. This is the most common protest reason.
  • Retaliatory driving: Spinning someone back because they hit you. Even if the first contact was accidental, if you retaliate intentionally, that's on you.
  • Blocking and brake-checking: Unsafe blocking or sudden brake applications to punish a trailing driver. Aggressive defense is legal; brake-checking is not.
  • Language and conduct: Abusive language toward other drivers in comments or voice chat is taken seriously by iRacing, as vulgar or harassing language results in driver suspension or a ban.
  • Unsporting conduct: Things like deliberately slow-driving to block faster drivers, gaming the caution system in ovals, or other actions that harm the integrity of the race.

The sporting code is long and detailed, and I've found that reading specific sections relevant to the series I race in (oval, road, dirt) actually helps me understand what's reportable. iRacing's website has the full code; it's worth skimming if you race competitively.

When to File a Protest (and When Not To)

Not every crash is protestable, and filing a frivolous protest wastes your time and iRacing's resources. I learned this the hard way by filing protests I later realized were just racing incidents. Here's how I think about it now:

File a protest if: The driver's actions appear deliberate and outside the bounds of hard racing. Intentional spinning, brake-checking, abusive language, or obvious retaliation are all clear cases. Get the replay file, note the lap and time of the incident, and describe what you saw.

Don't file a protest if: You were involved in a racing incident that could be interpreted as aggressive but not malicious. Contact was made, but it wasn't obviously intentional. The other driver didn't respond with retaliation. These are the gray areas that stewards often rule in favor of the driver being protested because intent is hard to prove from a replay alone.

Replay Perspective Check

Watch the replay in third-person view before filing. See if the driver's actions could reasonably be interpreted as accidental. If you can't rule out an accident, the stewards probably won't rule intentional conduct either.

How iRacing Investigates Protests

When you file a protest, iRacing's sporting officials review your replay file, read your description, and make a judgment call. They look for patterns: was this a one-off incident or part of a pattern of bad behavior by the protested driver? Drivers with previous violations for the same type of conduct face stiffer penalties.

The investigation process isn't transparent in real time. You file, and then you either get a notification that the protest was upheld or denied, usually within a few days. You don't see the steward's detailed reasoning unless it was a particularly high-profile case or you reach out to iRacing support.

One thing I've noticed: iRacing takes intentional conduct violations more seriously than simple racing contact. A driver who spins you out on lap 1 and then ignores you is more likely to face a penalty than a driver who makes aggressive contact that's borderline legal. Intent matters, and iRacing stewards are pretty good at spotting it from replays.

Sporting Code Penalties and License Impact

If your protest is upheld, the penalized driver faces consequences. These can range from a warning (first-time minor infractions) to license suspension or account closure (repeat violations or severe conduct breaches). Here's the general structure:

  1. First violation: Usually a warning. The driver is notified, but their license isn't affected.
  2. Repeat violations within a season: Penalties escalate to suspended license hours or a temporary suspension from racing.
  3. Severe or repeated violations: Extended license suspension (weeks or months) or permanent account closure in extreme cases (e.g., harassment, hate speech).

Your own safety rating and iRating can be affected by incidents you're involved in, even if you're the victim. That's why protecting your own conduct matters: don't retaliate, don't respond to bad sportsmanship with your own bad sportsmanship. Stay clean, file the protest if warranted, and let iRacing officials handle it.

What Happens If a Protest Is Filed Against You

I've had protests filed against me, and I can tell you it's stressful even when you know you didn't do anything wrong. If a protest is filed and you're the subject, you'll get a notification. iRacing will review the replay and sporting code violation claim, and you'll hear back with the outcome.

The best defense is clean racing. If you're accused of intentional contact but your replay shows you simply made an aggressive but legal pass, the stewards will likely dismiss the protest. If you intentionally spun someone and try to argue it was accidental, that is harder to defend because replays are pretty clear about driver intent.

Appeal Process Exists

If you disagree with a protest outcome against you, you can appeal or reach out to iRacing support with your own replay and context. Be professional and factual; do not blame the other driver or make excuses.

Protecting Your License: Practical Advice

The best protest strategy is to avoid being involved in situations that get protested in the first place. Here's what I focus on to stay clean:

  • Never retaliate in-race. You'll lose both the sporting code case and the protest case. Take the hit, finish the race, and file a protest if warranted.
  • Avoid aggressive blocking and brake-checking. Defensive driving is fine; sudden moves to punish trailing drivers are not.
  • Keep driver comments professional. Venting frustration in caps lock or with insults gets you reported. I learned this the hard way.
  • Know your series' rules. Different series have different expectations for contact. Road racing is tighter than oval racing. Dirt is different still.
  • File protests that are clear-cut. If you're unsure, you're probably wasting time. Focus on egregious violations that any steward would agree with.

Real Example: When I Filed and What Happened

In a Sportscar Endurance race I ran last season, a driver spun me out in a corner while I was clearly alongside. I watched the replay, and it was obvious he didn't see me and made a panicked move, but it was reckless and cost me the race position. I didn't retaliate. Instead, I filed a protest with the replay showing the incident, my car position, and a note that the contact was avoidable. iRacing upheld the protest, and the driver got a warning. That's how the system should work: documentation, evidence, and steward judgment.

FAQ

How long do I have to file a protest after a race?

iRacing typically allows protests to be filed within a few hours of the race ending, usually 2-4 hours depending on the series. Check the iRacing website or your race results page for the exact protest window. Don't wait; once the window closes, you can't protest the incident.

What evidence do I need to include when filing a protest?

Your replay file is the main piece of evidence. Include it along with a clear description of the incident: the lap number, the turn or section where it happened, what the driver did, and why you believe it violated the sporting code. Be specific and factual. Avoid emotional language or assumptions about intent unless the replay clearly shows it.

Can I protest someone for just being a bad driver or making a mistake?

No. Poor driving that results in accidental contact is not protestable. Protests are for conduct violations, intentional actions that breach the sporting code. Racing incidents, even ones that frustrate you, usually don't meet that threshold.

What happens to my iRating if I'm the victim of unsporting conduct?

Being spun out or hit doesn't directly affect your iRating. However, if you're involved in an incident (even as a victim), your safety rating may be impacted depending on the circumstances. The best approach is to drive cleanly, file protests when warranted, and focus on improving your own consistency and control on track.
