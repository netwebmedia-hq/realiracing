Here's what to expect from the NASCAR Cup Next Gen across the tracks you're likely to run: not a spec-sheet rundown, just a guide to how the car behaves and how to approach it.

Give a car a genuinely fair chance before forming an opinion on it: a session or two of just getting comfortable before you start comparing it to anything else you race. The NASCAR Cup Next Gen is a good example of a car that rewards that patience, since a first impression and an impression after a few weeks can be quite different.

What this car is really like to drive

The biggest adjustment coming from road racing is how much of the lap is about managing the car's balance change as fuel burns off and tires wear, rather than hitting a single perfect line lap after lap.

That character shows up most clearly once you're pushing near the limit rather than cruising around at seven-tenths, which is honestly true of most race cars; it's worth saying plainly here because a lot of first impressions of the NASCAR Cup Next Gen get formed in that first cautious session and then never get revisited.

Setup starting point

  • Wedge and track bar adjustments (where available) matter more on ovals than almost any setup parameter on a road course
  • Consider softer springs than you might expect going in, because oval setups reward compliance over the raw stiffness that helps on a smooth road course.
  • Managing tire temperature across a run is something to actively think about in a way road racing setups don't usually demand lap-to-lap

As with any setup notes, I'd treat these as a starting direction rather than a fixed recipe; track-specific demands, personal preference, and even the specific series rules you're racing under can all shift what actually works best. Change one thing at a time and judge it against a stopwatch, not just a feeling.

How it feels through the wheel

Oval racing puts less demand on the ~MOZA R9 for split-second direction changes and more on sustained, subtle cornering load; it is a genuinely different skill from road racing, and the wheel feedback reflects that difference clearly once you are a few laps in.

Pedal feel matters just as much here as wheel feel, and the same logic applies to load-cell pedals: a car's braking character comes through in how the pedal resists your foot, not just in how the car decelerates on screen. Cars with different weight distributions like the NASCAR Cup Next Gen genuinely reward paying attention to that resistance curve rather than just stomping to a fixed travel point every time.

Pro Tip

Don't judge your oval pace on lap one or two; Next Gen cars change character meaningfully as the tires and track come in, and the car that felt loose early can tighten up completely by lap ten.

Common mistakes in this car

  • Carrying over setup habits from a different car class instead of respecting what the NASCAR Cup Next Gen specifically rewards
  • Judging the car's limit too early, in the first session, before tires and the driver are both actually warmed up
  • Ignoring how the car's character changes over a longer run as tires wear, rather than just tuning for a single fast lap

Who this car suits

Road racers curious what oval racing actually demands, and anyone who wants to understand why NASCAR drivers talk about the car 'coming to them' over a run rather than just driving flat-out from lap one.

Practice plan for getting comfortable with this car

  1. Spend a full session with no other cars on track just learning where the NASCAR Cup Next Gen's grip limit actually is in isolation
  2. Run a handful of laps intentionally over the limit in a low-stakes session so you know what the car's warning signs feel like before you're relying on avoiding them under race pressure
  3. Do at least one longer run at race pace rather than qualifying pace before your first real race in the car, so tire wear and fuel load aren't a surprise
  4. Race it in a lower-pressure series first if you can, before bringing it into a series or split where the stakes and traffic density are higher

That progression matters more with the NASCAR Cup Next Gen than it might with a car you're already deeply familiar with, simply because every car has its own specific way of warning you before it lets go, and you can't actually learn that warning from reading about it; only from spending time right at the edge of it in a controlled setting.

What to change about your approach next time

A common mistake in the early sessions in the NASCAR Cup Next Gen is spending too long trying to force a setup and driving style that worked well in a different car onto it, rather than treating it as its own thing with its own demands from lap one. That can cost more time than any actual mechanical shortcoming in the car itself, and it's a mistake I think a lot of drivers make whenever they change car class: old habits are comfortable, but comfortable isn't the same as correct for the car you're actually in.

If you're starting out with this car, spend the first full session purely on getting comfortable with its specific braking and rotation character before touching the setup at all; changing the car's balance before you actually understand its default behavior muddies the feedback you're trying to learn from.

A quick note on iRating and Safety Rating in this car

Jumping into a new car class, including the NASCAR Cup Next Gen, resets some of your comfort even if your iRating and Safety Rating carry over. I'd expect a small dip in results for the first several races purely from unfamiliarity; it is a normal, temporary cost of learning something new, not a sign you've actually gotten worse at racing in general.
