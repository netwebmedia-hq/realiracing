How to Pick Your iRacing Series Each Season

Every iRacing season starts the same way for me. I open the schedule, see a wall of series, and feel the urge to try all of them. Two weeks later I have bought a car I drove once, a track pack for a series I never entered, and a half-finished plan for the series I actually wanted. This guide is the method I now use to avoid that, built on my own rig and my own habits. The numbers I mention are approximate and worth checking in your own account, since iRacing changes its schedule and store often.

A season is a fixed block, so plan it like one

iRacing runs in seasons of roughly 12 weeks, with a new schedule each time. That length matters. It is long enough to learn a car and short enough that a poor choice costs you only one block of racing. If you treat a season as a project with a start, a middle and an end, the decision about where to spend your time gets much easier.

Before opening the store, write down what you want from the next 12 weeks in a single sentence. Examples: 'finish races cleanly in a GT car', 'learn two new road courses', 'try ovals without wrecking anyone'. That sentence is the filter for every other choice in this article.

Start from the schedule you can actually race

The schedule is a calendar, not a menu. Look at the race times for each series you are considering and compare them with the nights you really have. A series with a fixed schedule that lands on a night you cannot race is a series you will skip, no matter how much you like the car.

  • Count the weeks where the race slots fit your life across the whole season.
  • Check whether the series has races at several times a day or only a few.
  • Note which weeks feature tracks you already know and which need preparation.
  • Skip any series where you would miss more than a third of the weeks.

Pick by license and safety rating, not by hype

Each series has a license requirement and a fixed or open setup. Your license class and safety rating decide what you can enter, and also which splits you will meet. If you want to understand how the ladder works, read our guide to the safety rating and the one on reaching Class D from the rookie series; both explain why a clean season is worth more than a fast one.

As a rule, I choose a series whose field is slightly above my comfort level but whose car I can already drive without fighting it. A car that is too fast produces incidents. A car that is too easy teaches little. The sweet spot is a car where the main learning is racecraft, not survival.

Count the content you own before you buy anything

iRacing lets you start with a limited free set of cars and tracks, and you buy the rest individually. That is the main place where budgets disappear, so I make a list before I open the store: which cars and tracks the series needs for all 12 weeks, and which of those I already own.

Then I sort the missing items into two groups. The first group is the content the series cannot be raced without, usually the car and the tracks that appear more than once in the schedule. The second group is nice-to-have, like a track that appears for a single week. I buy the first group only, and I leave the second for the weeks that feature those tracks, if I still feel like racing them.

One trick: many tracks appear in several series, and some cars are used across classes. Before you buy, search whether the track or car shows up in other places on your own schedule. Content that serves two series is worth more than content that serves one, and it is easy to miss when you only look at one series page.

Use one series as the anchor and one as the experiment

My rule is simple: one anchor series and, at most, one experiment. The anchor is where I put most of my races and most of my practice. The experiment is a series with a car or a discipline I am curious about, where I race only a handful of weeks and expect to be slow.

Having an anchor keeps your improvement visible. Because you race the same car on a rotating set of tracks, your results start to mean something. The experiment keeps the hobby fun without adding pressure, and it often reveals something that improves the anchor. A few weeks in an unfamiliar car can sharpen your braking in the one you race every night.

If you are not sure which class to anchor in, our comparison of GT3 and GT4 is a useful starting point, since the two classes ask for quite different things from the driver and from the wallet.

Plan the learning weeks as well as the race weeks

For each track in your anchor series, decide how many practice sessions you need before racing there. A track you know may need 20 minutes. A new one may need several evenings. If you want a method for the second case, see how to learn a new track, which breaks the process into small blocks that you can run in a normal week.

This is also where hardware starts to matter. On my rig, the MOZA R9 wheelbase and the CRP2 pedals give me consistent feedback and consistent brake feel, which makes it easier to compare one practice night with the next. A fixed aluminium cockpit adds to that, because nothing flexes between sessions. Specs vary by version and setup, so treat the numbers on the product pages as approximate and read the real settings in your own wheel software.

Set a review point at week six

Halfway through the season, ask three questions. Am I still enjoying the anchor series? Are my incident numbers moving in the right direction? Do I want to change the experiment? If two of those answers are negative, change something. You can swap the experiment series, reduce the number of weeks you race or switch the anchor for the next season.

Write the answers down. After two or three seasons the notes will show a pattern about what you like, what you tend to overbuy and which kinds of tracks cost you the most time. That pattern is more valuable than any ranking of series.

What I skip, on purpose

  • Buying a car because it looks great in a trailer.
  • Entering a series only because a streamer I follow races it.
  • Racing three series at once and learning none of them well.
  • Treating iRating as the only measure of a good season.

None of this requires a spreadsheet. A note on your phone with the sentence, the anchor, the experiment and the shopping list is enough. The point is to make the decision once, calmly, before the store and the schedule start making it for you.

A simple checklist to copy

  1. Write the one-sentence goal for the 12 weeks.
  2. Check which series fit the nights you can race.
  3. Filter by license, safety rating and car difficulty.
  4. List the content you need and the content you already own.
  5. Choose the anchor series and one experiment.
  6. Plan the practice nights for each new track.
  7. Mark week six in your calendar for a review.

Run through that list at the start of every season and the store becomes a place where you buy what you need, not a place where the schedule spends your money for you. If you have a method that works better for you, I would like to hear about it.

FAQ

How many iRacing series should I race in one season?

Most drivers improve fastest with one anchor series and at most one experiment. Racing more series than that spreads practice time across too many cars and tracks.

Do I need to buy every car and track for a series?

Usually not. Buy the car and the tracks that appear most often in the schedule first, and leave one-week tracks for later if you still want to race them.

How long is an iRacing season?

A season runs roughly 12 weeks, with a new schedule for each one. Check the official calendar for the exact dates, because they can shift.

When should I change series during a season?

A review around week six works well. If you are not enjoying the series or your incident numbers are getting worse, switch the experiment or reduce the number of weeks you race.
