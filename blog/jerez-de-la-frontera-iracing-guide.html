Jerez de la Frontera iRacing guide: Turn 1, curbing, racing lines

Jerez de la Frontera sits in southern Spain and plays host to some of the most satisfying corners in iRacing. The track flows fast from turn to turn. What catches drivers most is how quickly the circuit punishes small mistakes. A late apex at one corner feeds directly into a trail-braking zone for the next. The kerbs are usable but aggressive. One moment the racing line sits over the curbing, the next you are managing a late apex and holding a tight line through gravel run-off.

This guide covers the three critical sections where most lap time is either gained or lost: the opening fast-left at Turn 1, the technical Turn 4 complex, and the high-speed esses into the back straight. You will also find setup direction and a reference for consistent braking zones.

Turn 1 and the entry to the fast section

Turn 1 at Jerez is a right-hander that comes after the main straight. The corner itself is not sharp, but the entry is. Most drivers brake too late and run wide. The racing line requires you to brake while turning, trail the brakes through the middle of the corner, and then commit to the throttle early on the exit. The apex sits roughly at the inside kerb. There is a small raised edge there, and the best drivers clip it cleanly without damaging the suspension or losing steering authority.

The key is to brake in a straight line as long as possible. Most modern GT and open-wheel cars in iRacing can brake at full pressure until about 75 meters before the apex. Release pressure gradually as you turn in. Feel for the moment the car wants to understeer. That is your brake release point. If you wait too long, the rear slides wide. If you release too early, the front runs hot and the car pushes through the kerb. The threshold is narrow but learnable over a few laps.

Visual marker strategy

Once you find the brake release point at Turn 1, mark it with a visual reference on track. A shadow, a painted line, or a specific piece of scenery. Replicating that braking point lap after lap builds consistency faster than adjusting it every corner.

The Turn 4 complex and curb management

Turn 4 is a medium-speed left-hander that feeds into Turn 5, a quick right. This is where Jerez separates good drivers from fast ones. The approach looks straightforward. The execution demands precise curb placement. Turn 4 has a usable kerb on the exit. If you hit it flush and balanced, you gain two or three kph and set up a clean entry to Turn 5. If you tag it sideways or miss it entirely, you lose rotation through the next corner and waste a tenth.

The racing line for Turn 4 places the apex at the inside of the turn, just short of the painted kerb. You want to be steering toward the curb on exit, not away from it. Brake to a consistent speed before Turn 4. Most GT3 and GT2 cars do well with speeds in the range of 130 to 150 kph depending on fuel and tire wear. Trail the brakes lightly through the apex. The car should feel balanced, turning smoothly without needing steering lock. Hit the exit kerb square. This means your car is tracking toward the right line, not correcting into it. A square hit carries momentum. A sideways hit scrubs speed.

Turn 5 follows immediately. It is another right-hander but tighter than Turn 4. Do not aim at the curb yet. Instead, use the momentum from Turn 4 to rotate the car. Apex Turn 5 later than your instinct suggests. The corner opens up on exit. A late apex gives you more throttle room and a faster entry speed on the approach to the esses. Most drivers apexing Turn 5 too early will run out of curb on the exit and have nowhere to place the car if they run wide.

  • Turn 4: brake early, apex slightly before the kerb, carry speed to the exit kerb, hit it square and centered
  • Turn 5: rotate from Turn 4 momentum, late apex to open the corner, prioritize exit speed over mid-corner speed
  • Curb contact: aim for the upper flat face, not the angled edge. A flat contact is stable; an edge can deflect the car

The high-speed esses and back straight flow

The esses leading to the back straight are fast sweeping corners taken almost at full throttle. This section tests how well you manage front-end grip and curb placement at speed. The first ess (left turn) should be taken with minimal steering input. Most drivers brake slightly before this corner to load the front tire, then power through on throttle. The racing line sits near the inside kerb but not on it. You are using the curb as a reference point, not a guide. Approach this corner too hot and the front washes out. Too cautiously and you compromise exit speed.

The second ess (right turn) mirrors the first. This is where curbing becomes more useful. The inside curb is flat and wide. A controlled curb strike here actually aids rotation by giving the car a sense of a defined line. Many drivers enter the esses at the same speed but exit at different velocities because they manage the curb differently on the second ess. Clip the inside curb cleanly and you roll onto the back straight at maximum drive speed. Miss it or hit it awkwardly and you leave 2 to 3 kph on the table.

Sector-focused practice

Practice the esses in isolation during practice sessions. Run 10 consecutive laps through only that section, varying throttle position and curb placement. Log the data in telemetry software or use the delta bar to identify which lines produce the fastest segment times. Carving out this section well typically yields 0.3 to 0.5 seconds per lap.

Setup direction for consistency

Jerez rewards a setup that balances front-end responsiveness with rear stability. The track has frequent direction changes and heavy braking zones, so tire temperature management matters more than raw grip. A common baseline is a slightly softer front anti-roll bar (ARB) than rear. This helps the car turn into the fast corners without asking the rear to work too hard under braking. Too soft a front and the car will understeer on corner entry. Too stiff and the tires will go off in temperature quickly.

Brake pressure tuning is often the biggest lap-time lever at Jerez. Most drivers benefit from a brake bias shift toward the front (roughly 60 to 65% front, 35 to 40% rear) because of the consistent trail-braking zones. However, test this in practice. If the rear locks under heavy braking into Turn 1 or Turn 4, move bias rearward slightly. If the front tire temperature rises faster than the rear during a practice session, your bias may be too far forward. Suspension stiffness on the rear can also help stabilize the car under trail-braking if you find the rear sliding.

Steering lock angle should be set to match the sharpest turn on track. At Jerez, that is likely Turn 5 or one of the later technical corners. Most GT3 cars run 20 to 25 degrees of lock. Too much lock and the car feels sluggish in faster corners. Too little and you will run out of steering adjustment in the tight spots. Adjust in small increments and test over multiple laps before committing to a change.

  1. Start with a baseline anti-roll bar setting from your car's stock setup or the official fixed setup
  2. Run 5 laps and check tire temperatures. Front tires warmer than rear by more than 5 degrees means softening the front ARB
  3. Test brake bias starting at 60% front, 40% rear. Increase front bias by 2 to 3% if the rear locks during trail-braking
  4. Verify steering lock against the sharpest corner. Add 1 to 2 degrees if you reach maximum lock while still turning; remove 1 degree if steering input feels overly sensitive

Braking zones and throttle points

Consistency at Jerez comes from replicating braking points. The main straights into Turn 1 and Turn 9 (the chicane before the back straight) are the two heaviest braking zones. Use the same visual marker each lap. The straight from Turn 11 leading back to Turn 1 is long enough to see the apex of Turn 1 as you approach. Many drivers use the moment the apex curb comes into clear focus as their braking point. This is roughly 110 to 120 meters out depending on your car and tire wear.

Throttle application happens earlier at Jerez than at tracks like Spa or Silverstone because the corners are tighter and closer together. Often you are already at full throttle only seconds after reaching the apex. This means exit speed from each corner directly affects your entry speed to the next. A tenth of a second gained on the Turn 4 exit becomes real lap time because you enter Turn 5 and the esses faster. Conversely, poor turn-out will cascade through the next two corners. Focus on exit speed and curb placement before worrying about mid-corner speed adjustments.

Wet weather strategy at Jerez

When rain arrives, the fast sections (the esses and early corners) become the most treacherous. The track is smooth and polished in these areas. Wet tires struggle to generate grip on those slick surfaces. In contrast, the technical Turn 4 and Turn 5 complex becomes relatively safer because the car is traveling slower and the asphalt has more texture. Adjust brake pressure 5 to 10% rearward in the wet and increase your trail-braking window slightly. The rear will lock faster on slick surfaces when the car is in a high-speed corner.

The chicane at Turn 9 deserves extra caution in the rain. It is a quick double-apex section that demands smooth steering input. Any abrupt movement will cause a slide. Smooth throttle application and gentle steering angle changes are the rule. If the track is shedding water or if standing water is visible near the outside line of Turn 5 or Turn 9, avoid those areas. Plan your racing line to keep the car on the dryer parts of the track.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

  • Braking too late into Turn 1: costs you the opportunity to trail-brake smoothly. Solution: brake at your marked visual point and accept a slightly slower mid-corner speed to gain a cleaner exit
  • Missing the exit curb at Turn 4: this sounds minor but costs 0.2 seconds per lap over a stint. Spend practice laps specifically aiming for that curb contact
  • Overslowing the esses: many drivers brake harder than needed before the esses, then have to make up speed on the back straight where they run out of track. Solution: trust the grip, carry speed, adjust curb placement instead of throttle
  • Inconsistent brake pressure: changing how hard you brake from lap to lap will never reveal your true pace. Set brake bias and pressure in practice and hold it steady during racing

Jerez rewards smooth, precise inputs above all else. The kerbs are there to use, not to avoid, but they demand respect. Spend your first practice session simply getting comfortable with the curb placement on each major turn. Use the second session to dial in brake zones and throttle application. By race day, the track will feel predictable and you will be able to focus on tire management and racing your competitors instead of wrestling with the line.

FAQ

What is the hardest corner at Jerez for most drivers?

Turn 4 and Turn 5 together. The pair demands precise curb placement and rotation through both corners in sequence. Missing the exit curb at Turn 4 or apexing Turn 5 too early will cascade into a poor entry to the esses, costing significant lap time. The technical difficulty is higher than Turn 1 even though Turn 1 looks more intimidating.

Should I use the curbs at Jerez or avoid them?

Use them strategically. The exit curb at Turn 4, the second-ess curb, and the apex curb at Turn 1 are designed to be part of the racing line. Hit them square and centered on the flat part, not on an angled edge. Avoid the curb at Turn 5 exit and the outside curbs of the esses because they can destabilize the car. Curb contact should feel natural and aid momentum, not feel like an obstacle.

What brake bias setting is a good starting point at Jerez?

Begin with 60 percent front, 40 percent rear. Jerez has continuous trail-braking zones which typically favor more front bias than other tracks. If your rear tire locks under braking into Turn 1 or Turn 4, move bias rearward by 2 to 3 percent. If front temperatures spike during a stint, your bias is too aggressive. Adjust in small increments over several laps before making bigger changes.

How much time can I gain from improving my curb placement at Jerez?

Depending on your current technique, improving curb management can yield 0.3 to 0.8 seconds per lap. The exit curb at Turn 4, the curb work through the esses, and the apex curb at Turn 1 are the three highest-impact areas. Most drivers lose time here because they either miss the curb or hit it at an angle, scrubbing speed instead of maintaining it. Dedicated practice on curb placement specifically can pay dividends quickly.
