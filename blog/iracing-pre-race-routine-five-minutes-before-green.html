My iRacing pre-race routine: the five minutes before green

Qualifying ends, the timer rolls into the pre-race countdown, and I realise I never looked at the fuel number. The brake bias is still where I left it after practice in a different car. Nothing has gone wrong yet, but the car I am about to race is slightly off, and I will feel that for the whole race.

My routine fits in about five minutes and I run it in the same order every time. The order matters more than any single item, because a fixed sequence makes a missing step obvious. The rig is a MOZA R9 wheelbase, MOZA CRP2 pedals and a fixed aluminium cockpit, so some checks lean on that hardware, but the sequence works on any setup.

What happens between qualifying and the grid

The result lands and the instinct is to stare at where you qualified. I give it a few seconds and move on, because the grid position is already decided and the next few minutes are not. Depending on the series and the session, there is a gap before the cars are gathered on the grid. That gap is the only quiet time you get, and everything below happens inside it.

Whether you can still change the car in that gap depends on the series. In an open-setup series I can load a different setup. In a fixed-setup series there is nothing to change, and I still open the garage screen to confirm what the car is carrying.

The car first

Fuel and setup

I check the fuel load against a plan I made earlier, not against a feeling. If I ran a long stint in practice, I know roughly what a lap burns. If I did not, I add a margin and accept starting a little heavy. A heavy car in the first laps costs less than doing fuel arithmetic with the race on the line.

Then the setup. The name of the loaded setup is right there in the garage, and I read it out loud. Carrying a qualifying setup into the race is an easy mistake, and so is loading a setup saved for a different track. It takes two seconds to check and a whole race to regret.

Brake bias starting point

I set brake bias to the number in my own notes for that car, not a number from a forum thread. It is a starting point for the first lap, when the tyres are cold and the car is full of fuel and I do not want to be adjusting anything. If you want the mechanics of why the number moves during a run, the brake bias article covers it.

I also confirm I can reach the adjustment without looking. A bias knob or button that I fumble for on lap one is worse than a slightly wrong bias.

Wheel and pedals

FFB strength check

Force feedback strength that felt right in one car can clip in another. A heavy car with high steering loads will saturate a wheelbase on a setting that felt perfect in a light car, and clipped force feedback hides the detail you need in the first corners. The R9 is rated at ~9 Nm peak, which is plenty of headroom, but headroom only helps if the setting suits the car.

I keep a note of the strength I use per car type and I check it against the car on the grid. Turning the wheel slowly through its range while stationary tells me whether the weight feels like what I expect. For the underlying ideas, my piece on force feedback goes through clipping and gain in detail. I save experiments for practice and use the grid only to confirm.

Pedal check

I press each pedal to full travel once and watch the input display. Throttle reaches full, brake reaches full under a hard press, and the clutch, if the car uses one, does the same. The CRP2 brake is load cell based, so what I am checking is that the pressure I give produces the full bar I expect. A loose bolt or a shifted plate shows up here, not at the first braking zone.

A fixed cockpit means the pedals do not walk around under load, and I still check my foot position and the seat distance. Cramped legs in the second half of a race start as a small annoyance on the grid.

Screen and audio

Black box layout

I pick one black box layout for the start and leave it alone. Relative and fuel are the two I want on screen for the opening laps, with timing details left for later. Cycling through boxes on lap one means my eyes are inside the cockpit while the pack is bunched, and that is the worst possible moment for it.

Spotter volume

The spotter needs to be audible over engine noise and still not startle me on every call. I test it before the start by listening for the first call and adjusting from there. In the first corners the spotter is half my awareness of the cars beside me, so a spotter set too quiet is a real handicap.

Who is around you

The entry list shows iRating and safety rating beside every name. I look at the cars directly ahead and behind me on the grid and nothing else. A driver with a low safety rating next to me gets extra room in the first corners. A car far above my iRating starting behind me will probably be quicker, and I would rather let it go than fight it into turn two.

These numbers are a prior and the first corner will update them. A low rating can belong to someone fast and new, and a high one can belong to someone having a bad day. I use the numbers to decide how much space to leave, and the actual driving to adjust.

Turn one in your head

I run the first corner in my head once or twice: where the pack will bunch, where the braking marker is, and where I turn in if the car ahead brakes early. Then I pick an escape line. That means a specific piece of track I will aim for if the corner closes up, whether that is the outside, the run-off or a gap on the inside.

Choosing it now takes the decision out of the moment. When everything happens at once, a pre-chosen line is faster than a good one found on the fly. The launch itself is a separate skill, and I wrote about it in race start technique.

Notifications, hands, breath

With a few minutes left, I silence everything. Phone on do-not-disturb, chat apps closed, desktop notifications off. One pop-up in the corner of the screen during the start is enough to cost a position or a car.

Then hands off the wheel. I rest them on my thighs, drop my shoulders and take a few slow breaths. Grip tension is what I am trying to shed, because a direct drive wheelbase passes on everything in your hands, including tension. I put my hands back on the wheel just before the cars roll, relaxed rather than gripping.

When you join late

Sometimes the session is already running when you arrive, after a slow load or a disconnect. The full routine does not fit, so I cut it to what matters for safety and carry on.

  1. Pedals: one full press of throttle and brake to confirm they register.
  2. Brake bias: set to my starting number.
  3. Fuel and setup: a glance at both in the garage if there is time.
  4. FFB: adjust on the first slow laps if it feels wrong.
  5. Everything else waits until the field has settled.

If the field is already moving, I stay off the racing line and let faster cars pass cleanly. Chasing to make up for lost time is how a late join becomes a crash in the first corner. A slow first lap costs a few seconds and a crash costs the race.

Practical tip

Write the routine on a card and tape it inside the cockpit where you can see it from the seat. A written order is easier to follow than one you try to remember while the countdown is running.

FAQ

How long should an iRacing pre-race routine take?

Mine takes about five minutes, and the exact length matters less than running it in the same order every time. A fixed order makes a skipped step obvious. If you are short on time, cut the extras and keep the checks that affect safety: pedals, brake bias, fuel and the loaded setup.

Should I change brake bias before the race starts?

I set it to the starting number from my own notes for that car and leave it alone for the first lap. The tyres are cold and the car is heavy, so lap one is a poor time to experiment. I adjust once the car has settled and I know what it is doing.

Is it worth checking the entry list before the start?

Yes, briefly. iRating and safety rating are visible beside each name, and the cars directly ahead and behind me on the grid are the ones that matter. The numbers tell me how much room to leave in the first corners. They are a starting guess, and the driving itself updates it.

What should I do if I join an iRacing session late?

Skip the full routine and do the safety items: a pedal press, the brake bias and a glance at fuel and setup. Stay off the racing line and let faster cars pass. Adjust force feedback or anything else on the first slow laps once the field has settled.
