Fuji Speedway iRacing Guide: Lines, Setup, Wet Weather

Fuji Speedway sits at ~600 meters elevation in Japan's Shizuoka Prefecture, and that altitude changes everything about how your car feels. The track climbs through high-speed corners, then drops hard into technical braking zones. Fuji punishes patience; you have to commit early and trust the grip, especially through Turns 1 and 2 where the road rises beneath you. This guide draws on published specs and common setup practice, not a hands-on test of every product. It covers the racing line, brake points, and the setup adjustments to make, plus force feedback advice for a MOZA R9 rig, to handle both the fast dry sessions and the tricky wet-weather racing that catches a lot of drivers out.

Track Layout and Racing Line

Fuji is a ~4.6 km circuit with 16 corners, and the topography is the defining feature. You climb from Turn 1 (the uphill left-hander) all the way to Turn 7 (Spoon Curve), then drop back down through the technical lower portion. The racing line philosophy differs significantly between the upper and lower sections.

  • Turn 1 (Turns 1–2 complex): Brake hard on the straight, enter late and slow for the uphill left, then immediately turn right into Turn 2. Stay tight to the apex; the elevation gain means the car wants to run wide. Early throttle out of Turn 2 pays dividends.
  • Turns 3–5 (Dunlop Curve complex): Turn 3 is a fast downhill left. Turn 4 is a quick right, and Turn 5 is another left. Chain these together smoothly: trail brake into Turn 3 to keep front-end load, clip the apex tight at Turn 4, then build speed into Turn 5.
  • Turns 6–7 (Spoon Curve): Turn 6 is a medium-speed right-hander; Turn 7 (Spoon) is the iconic fast left at the top of the circuit. You'll carry 180+ km/h through Spoon if you've nailed your entry. This is where elevation helps: the car rides on the crest naturally.
  • Turns 8–9–10 (The Drop): After Spoon, the track falls away steeply. Turn 8 is a right, Turn 9 is a tight left (100R), and Turn 10 is another right. This is the slowest part of the lap and demands precise braking. Trail brake deep into Turn 8 because the car loses grip on the descent.
  • Turns 11–12 (200R complex): A pair of fast, sweeping lefts downhill. Carry momentum; this is where you gain time. Early power out of Turn 12 sets you up for the final sector.
  • Turns 13–14–15–16 (Final sector): Fast stuff. Turn 13 is a sweeping left (130R), Turn 14 a right, Turn 15 a quick left, and Turn 16 a fast right back onto the main straight. Smooth inputs matter more than aggression here.
Elevation as a Setup Tool

The elevation change between Turn 1 and Turn 7 is roughly 100 meters. Use this to your advantage: carry extra speed through the top section because gravity helps you scrub speed on the way down. Brake later into Turn 8 than you think you need to.

Brake Points and Downshift Strategy

Fuji's brake points are aggressive because most corners come after high-speed sections. Consistent pedal feel matters here, and load-cell pedals such as the MOZA CRP2 are one option, because pressure feedback is critical when small miscalculations compound over a lap.

  • Turn 1: Brake around the 200m board, aiming for ~100 km/h entry. This is the most important corner for lap time; nail it and the rest flows. The uphill gradient works against you; don't expect the car to stop as quickly as at sea-level tracks.
  • Turn 3: Brake around 100m board for ~150 km/h. Trail brake all the way through because you're descending; the car will continue to slow naturally.
  • Turn 8: Brake around 75m board for ~90 km/h. This is where most drivers lock up or miss the apex. The elevation drop makes the front-end light; modulate carefully.
  • Turn 13 (130R): Minimal braking; treat it as a high-speed flow corner. Most of your speed loss comes from steering input, not pedal.

For downshifts, use a rev-matching technique through the braking zones to keep the engine engaged and reduce turbo lag on corner exit. The downshift from 4th to 3rd into Turn 1 needs to happen early (around 150 km/h) so you're settled by the apex. Into Turn 8, downshift in two stages (4th → 3rd → 2nd) to avoid overwhelming the rear on the descent.

Dry Setup: Balance and Downforce

Fuji's fast uphill sections demand mechanical grip, so lean toward a stiffer setup than you'd run on a flat circuit. The downhill portions need compliance to avoid unsettling the car. Here's a baseline to start from:

  • Anti-roll bars: Front ~12, Rear ~10. The stiffer front helps Turn 1; the slightly softer rear prevents the car from pitching through the drops.
  • Springs: Front ~120 N/m, Rear ~110 N/m. Stiff enough to support the quick direction changes, but not so stiff you lose feel in the braking zones.
  • Brake bias: ~58% front. Fuji has good grip, so you can run a bit more rear brake than at most circuits. Trail braking is smoother with balanced bias.
  • Downforce: Start at ~70% front, ~60% rear if the car is being raced in a GT series. The speed carries you through Spoon and 130R, so don't cut downforce to gain straightline speed because the cornering loss isn't worth it.
  • Differential: 75% accel, 65% decel. Fuji's exits (especially Turn 2 and Turn 12) reward a slightly aggressive diff for early throttle application.

The key adjustment is damper tuning. Fuji's bumps are real but not violent; set bump damping to ~8 clicks from the soft end, rebound to ~6. This keeps the car settled without wallowing in the high-speed stuff. If you're running VR and notice a lot of pitch during braking into Turn 8, that's usually damper-related, not spring-related.

Wet-Weather Driving at Fuji

Fuji in the wet is genuinely treacherous. The elevation changes mean standing water can appear in unexpected places (especially through the lower section), and the painted sections (track limits lines, pit entry) become skating rinks. Treat wet Fuji like a completely different track.

  • Turn 1 and Turn 2: These become your first major wet-weather challenge. The uphill approach is slippery; don't carry pace into the braking zone. Brake at the same board as dry, but apply ~70% of the pressure you'd normally use. Let the car settle at the apex before rolling throttle.
  • Spoon Curve: In light rain, this is manageable. In heavy rain, it becomes a lottery. The crest loses front-end grip unpredictably. Run a tighter line (closer to the inside) and lift slightly mid-corner if you feel the front wash. Accept that you won't carry the same speed.
  • The Drop (8–10): This is where Fuji bites hardest in the wet. Turn 8's descent is deceptive; your braking point moves back by ~50m because the descending road robs traction. Many drivers lock the fronts here. Brake earlier, use less pedal, and trail brake through the corner with patience.
  • Painted surfaces: Avoid them entirely. The pit entry line, track-limits paint, and the rumble strips become ice in the wet. Plan your line to stay on asphalt.

For wet setup, soften the springs by ~10%, increase damper rebound to help the car escape water with a shorter response cycle, and reduce brake bias to ~52% front because the rear is more likely to lock in the wet, so you need margin. Lower downforce by 20% to reduce overall load on the tires. Lean on the force feedback in the wet to feel the car's confidence through each corner.

Wet-Weather Patience

Fuji's wet-weather difficulty comes from overconfidence. Most drivers try to carry too much speed into the lower section. Treat the drop (Turns 8–10) as the slowest part of your lap; if you're within 2–3 seconds of your wet pace by Turn 10, you're doing well. Build pace gradually.

Force Feedback and Steering Feel

Fuji's elevation and changing grip levels mean the FFB profile matters. On a wheelbase like the R9 (~9 N·m), aim for a setup that emphasizes mid-range feedback over extreme peak torque. Set the sensitivity to ~90%, and reduce any in-game vibration so you're reading the road, not being rattled.

  • Turn 1 (uphill left): You'll feel the front-end loading as the car climbs. It's intentional feedback; trust it. If the feedback disappears suddenly, you've run out of grip.
  • Spoon Curve: The crest creates a moment of weightlessness mid-corner. Your wheel will feel lighter; this is normal and not a setup problem. Maintain your steering angle and throttle position through this light spot.
  • Turns 8–10: The descending road creates off-center feedback as the car pitches forward. Use this as your braking-zone reference. When the wheel's feedback suggests settling, you can begin throttle application.
  • 130R: This fast left-hander should feel smooth and controlled. If it's twitchy, your setup is too stiff or your brake bias is too aggressive. Soften the front ARB by 1–2 clicks.

Common Mistakes and How to Fix Them

  • Overbraking into Turn 1: The elevation makes this corner feel like you need more speed than you actually do. You'll lock the fronts or miss the apex. Practice the brake point methodically; don't chase pace.
  • Too much throttle out of Turn 2: The car will push wide because you're still climbing. Patience through Turn 2 means a much better exit into Turn 3.
  • Locking up through the Drop: This is the most common crash point. The descending road surprises drivers. Brake earlier and use less pressure; trust the brake zone to be longer in the wet.
  • Running hot tire temperatures: Fuji's speed through the fast sections can cook the fronts. If you're blistering front tires by lap 5, run slightly lower downforce, increase brake bias rearward to use the rears more, and check your steering lock; excessive lock means excess friction.

Practice Routine and Benchmarks

If you're learning Fuji for the first time, structure your practice like this: spend 10 laps learning the racing line at 80% throttle, focusing on Turn 1 and the Spoon–Drop transition. These are where the car's behavior changes most. Then spend 10 laps working on brake points, increasing pressure gradually until you're at competitive pace. Finally, do 10-lap race simulations to build consistency.

A decent baseline lap in a GT3 car is around 1:35 for a driver with solid racecraft. If you're running 1:38+, you're likely overbraking or losing too much speed through the fast stuff. Check your FFB settings on the R9 to make sure you're reading the track correctly, then focus on trusting the grip through Spoon and 130R.

Final Thoughts

Fuji rewards commitment and patience in equal measure. The elevation changes mean your car setup and braking strategy need to adapt throughout the lap in ways flat circuits don't demand. Spend time understanding how the rise to Spoon affects your driving, then how the fall to the chicane changes grip. Once you're comfortable with that rhythm, Fuji becomes one of the most satisfying circuits to push on because the feedback is immediate and honest. The R9's torque strength gives you excellent FFB clarity through the technical lower section, so lean on that fine control to build your confidence lap by lap.

FAQ

What's the correct racing line through Spoon Curve?

Spoon Curve (Turn 7) is a fast left-hander at the top of the circuit. Brake around the 150m board coming off Turn 6, then turn in early to apex midway through the corner. The key is that the road crests at the apex, so the car will feel light; do not panic and do not add more steering input. Carry it flat out (or very near it) through the corner, then progressive throttle back down the descent into Turn 8. The faster you're willing to commit through Spoon, the better your lap.

Why does my car push in Turn 1 on the uphill?

The uphill gradient loads the front tires, but it also changes how quickly the car slows down. Most drivers brake too late because the visual perspective is deceptive; the road rising ahead makes the corner feel farther away than it is. Try braking at the 200m board and focus on hitting the apex tight, even if it feels slow. Also check your brake bias: if it's over 60% front, you might be locking the inner wheel on the initial turn-in. Dial it back to 58% and try again.

How do I manage the descent from Spoon through Turn 10?

This section feels fast and dangerous because the elevation drop makes your car feel lighter than it is. The trick is to treat it as a series of small, controlled inputs rather than one big save. Let the car breathe: brake hard into Turn 8, stay smooth with steering angle and throttle through Turns 9–10, then gradually build speed as the road flattens. If you're locking up or spinning, you're either braking too late or not using enough trail-brake technique to manage the front-end load on descent. Practice trail braking to hold the front-end stable as the road drops.

What setup changes should I make for wet Fuji?

Soften springs by ~10%, reduce brake bias from 58% to 52% to prevent rear-wheel locking, lower downforce by about 20%, and increase damper rebound slightly to help the car escape standing water. The biggest change is mental: Fuji in heavy rain is slippery and demands patience through the drop section. Accept that you'll be ~5–7 seconds slower per lap than dry pace. The painted track-limits lines are treacherous, so plan your line to avoid them entirely.
