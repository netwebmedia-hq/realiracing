Circuit Paul Ricard iRacing guide: chicane choice and Mistral straight

Circuit Paul Ricard sits in the south of France with a layout that punishes indecision. The most visible puzzle for first-time visitors is the chicane complex before the Mistral Straight, where two legal racing lines exist and choosing the wrong one or executing it poorly can cost over a tenth of a second per lap. Add the high-speed flow of the Mistral and the technical challenge of holding a stable line through Virage de la Signes after it, and Paul Ricard demands precision across three separate skills: line selection, throttle discipline, and mid-corner speed.

This guide covers the strategic choice between the two chicane options, how to brake late into Turn 1 and maintain momentum, and how to exit the chicane so cleanly that the Mistral straight becomes a confidence-building section rather than a white-knuckle gamble.

Understanding the two chicane lines

Paul Ricard's chicane is unique: both the right-hander and left-hander before it form a quick S that can be driven two ways. The inside line runs tighter through both apexes. The outside line sweeps wider and straighter. Both are legal, both are raced, and which one works depends on your car, your braking feel, and the traffic state.

The inside line is narrower and requires sharper steering inputs. It sacrifices some mid-corner speed through both turns but compresses the lateral distance traveled, so if you carry a lot of entry speed or run a looser car setup, you stay more stable. The outside line is faster if you can manage the extended entry and longer exit phase, but it demands higher mid-corner speeds and a car setup that turns well without the driver having to fight it.

  • Inside line: tighter radius turns, lower mid-corner speed, more consistent on looser platforms, slower absolute exit speed but smaller margin for error at entry
  • Outside line: wider radius, higher mid-corner speed, requires a well-balanced car, faster absolute exit if nailed, but one botched turn costs more than the inside line mistake
Line Selection Strategy

Test both lines in practice at 50% race pace before choosing one for qualifying. A small setup change, such as adding front wing or adjusting brake bias, can swing the speed advantage from one line to the other. Settle on one before the race.

Braking, turn-in, and the entry phase

The chicane begins after a modest straight from Virage de la Signes. Most drivers brake too early out of habit. The target braking point is the inside edge of the white kerb line on the right. Braking much earlier than that forces you to coast, which robs exit speed. Braking later than the kerb demands a sharper downshift and risks locking the fronts.

Your goal is to trail-brake: the brake pedal should still be releasing as you turn in. The car should feel stable and heavy, not light or drifty. If it feels twitchy on entry, you are either turning in too early, braking too late, or both. Adjust both inputs together. Late trail-braking into a chicane is the opposite of a panic move. It is a control move.

Once committed to turn-in, hold the steering angle steady for one or two frames. Many drivers oversteer here by continuing to add lock. The car will settle into the apex naturally if you let it. The right-hander is shallower than the left, so your steering angle to the left should be sharper.

  1. Brake at the inside edge of the kerb or slightly before
  2. Release the brake gradually as steering angle increases
  3. Set steering angle early and hold it stable
  4. Squeeze the throttle when the car feels planted at apex, not before

The apex phase and transitions

The first apex is where many drivers lose the lap. They turn in, but their speed is still too high, so they run wide or understeer out. The first right-hander should feel planted and direct. The apex is the inner edge of the kerbing. Run over it or just kiss it. Do not aim for smooth asphalt unless you are running the outside line, in which case your apex is the tip of the white striping.

The transition from right to left is short and critical. Your car will be at mid-to-high lateral load on the right turn. You have less than a second to unwind the steering and turn the opposite direction. If you carry too much speed through the right, the car will fight you on the transition and you will either understeer across the white transition zone or lock the inside rear and spin. If you carry the right speed, the car breathes through the transition smoothly.

The left-hander is tighter than the right. Your steering angle should be greater. The apex is also the inner kerb. Most drivers are timid here because they worry about running over the left-side striping and damaging the car, but running over white paint causes no penalty at Paul Ricard in most series. Trust the apex location and turn in firmly.

Throttle application and exit speed

Exit speed from the left-hander sets your speed onto the Mistral Straight. This is where the outside line can gain time if driven cleanly. Because the outside line has a wider turn radius, the car can be pointed further down the straight earlier. You can begin throttle application while still turning, so you exit with momentum and carry more speed into the Mistral.

On the inside line, you are pointed down the straight later, so you wait a beat longer before applying full throttle. But the inside line is more stable in a loose car, so if setup or confidence is an issue, this trade is worth it.

Do not apply throttle while the car is still in yaw. Wait until the steering wheel is straight. Doing so early will either spin you or scrub speed. A common beginner mistake is standing on the throttle before the car is pointed right, which wastes energy fighting the lateral forces instead of accelerating forward.

Gear as a Speed Gauge

Count the gear at apex. Most cars take this chicane in third gear. If you are downshifting to second, your entry speed is too slow. If you are in fourth at apex, your entry speed is too high and you will understeer.

The Mistral Straight and Virage de la Signes

The Mistral Straight is the longest on the circuit, roughly 1800 meters. Conventional wisdom says to aim for the widest entry and exit at maximum speed. That is backwards for mid-pack traffic. In clean air, a smooth chicane exit and steady throttle application at full aperture will accelerate you continuously down the straight. In traffic, your line must accommodate a dive-bomb or a weaker car alongside.

Virage de la Signes, the right-hander at the end of Mistral, is a 130+ mph turn on most setups. It is flat-out or nearly so. The apex is the inner kerb. Do not trail-brake here. Brake hard before the turn-in point, release the brake fully before steering, then maintain throttle to the apex and beyond. Any lift of throttle mid-corner will upset the car. Any brake mid-corner will lock the fronts.

The exit of Virage de la Signes leads directly to braking for the chicane again. If you run too wide on the exit, you will apex the chicane from a bad angle and spend the next two turns working out the mistake. Run the inside kerb on exit, even though it feels tight. The payoff is a direct line into the next chicane.

Setup tuning for the chicane and Mistral

Paul Ricard punishes cars that are either too loose or too stiff. The chicane demands stability and turn-in response. Virage de la Signes demands mid-corner grip. Most baseline setups will work, but small adjustments pay off.

  • Too loose through the chicane: add front wing, stiffen front anti-roll bar slightly, reduce front ride height to increase downforce
  • Too tight (understeering): reduce front wing, soften front anti-roll bar, add front ride height
  • Car is drifty in Virage de la Signes: add downforce, stiffen suspension, increase brake balance toward front to reduce rotation
  • Locking the fronts under chicane entry: reduce brake bias toward rear, soften brake pressure
  • Difficult to maintain Mistral straight line in traffic: reduce wing, accept lower top speed for better stability in car-to-car proximity

Traffic management and overtaking

The chicane is a natural bottleneck. Most overtakes happen either before it or after Virage de la Signes into the braking zone. Attempting a pass through the chicane itself is low-probability and high-crash. If you are faster and the car ahead is loose through the chicane, pass him before it on the Mistral approach or wait for Virage and brake later on the next sequence.

If you are defending the chicane, hold your preferred line firmly. Do not move to block. Move only once, if at all, before turn-in. After turn-in, your job is to stay on your line and leave no room. An attacker who thinks there is space will take risks and crash himself.

On the Mistral Straight itself, a slipstream advantage is real but not decisive. You may gain 2-3 kph in the draft, but that translates to a tenth of a second over the full straight. Attempting a daring pass at the end of Mistral into Virage de la Signes is usually a crash or a black flag. A patient inside-line pass into the chicane at the start of the next lap costs you a moment but keeps you on track.

Common mistakes and fixes

  • Braking too early into the chicane and coasting: You are robbing exit speed. Move the brake point deeper and trust your car. Brake only as much as necessary.
  • Oversteering the entry: You are turning in too sharply or too early. Wait for the car to settle, then turn in smoothly. Smooth is faster.
  • Running over the white striping on the transition: Legal at Paul Ricard on most series. The striping is part of the track. Use it.
  • Applying throttle while the car is still turning: This spins you or scrubs speed. Wait until steering is straight, then squeeze the throttle.
  • Understeering through Virage de la Signes: You are on the brakes too long or too hard mid-corner. Trail-brake harder at entry, release fully before turn-in.
  • Overshooting the chicane width: The chicane is defined. Stay inside the white lines. Running wide is a track-limits violation on many series.
Practice with Real Race Conditions

Practice the chicane at race pace with cold tyres and fuel. Many drivers learn the chicane at high pace with hot rubber and a light tank, then struggle in qualifying when fuel load and tyre temperature are different. Get comfortable with the entry and exit across fuel and tyre states.

Building consistency lap after lap

The chicane and Mistral straight account for roughly 15% of the lap time. Nailing them consistently is the difference between a safe setup that runs 1:54 every lap and a sharp setup that does 1:53:2 on lap one, 1:53:8 on lap two, and then crashes on lap five. Consistency is the goal. Choose one line, one braking point, one throttle application, and repeat it 50 times. Build the muscle memory. On race day, the chicane becomes automatic and your attention can shift to traffic, fuel, and tire management.

FAQ

Which chicane line is faster at Paul Ricard?

The outside line is faster in absolute terms if nailed cleanly because it allows higher mid-corner speeds and earlier throttle application out of the left-hander. The inside line is more forgiving and often faster in the hands of a driver who is uncomfortable with the car or still learning the track. Test both in practice and choose based on your car setup, confidence, and the traffic state on race day.

How do I stop understeering through the chicane?

Reduce your entry speed by braking later and harder, or run less front wing. Understeer at the chicane is almost always an entry speed problem, not a braking problem. If you are locked onto the brakes until turn-in, you cannot slow down more. Brake earlier and with less pedal pressure, then release the brake before you steer. This gives the front tyres grip budget for turning.

What gear should I be in at the chicane apex?

Most cars take the chicane in third gear. If you are in second gear at the apex, your entry speed is too low and you are costing yourself exit speed and acceleration down the Mistral. If you are in fourth, your entry speed is too high and you will understeer. Adjust your braking point and entry speed until you hit third gear consistently at the apex.

Is Virage de la Signes flat-out or do I need to lift?

Virage de la Signes is near flat-out or fully flat-out depending on your car, setup, fuel load, and tyre condition. A heavy car with low downforce may need a small lift. A light car with high downforce can stay at full throttle. The rule is: brake hard before the turn-in point, release the brake fully before steering, then stay on the throttle. Any mid-corner lift will upset the car and lose time. If you are locking up under braking, increase brake balance toward the rear or soften pedal pressure.
